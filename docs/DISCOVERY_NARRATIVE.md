# Discovery Narrative: From Alert Noise to Governance

> Figures below are computed live from `data/warehouse/governance.duckdb` and describe a **synthetic** banking scenario, not a real one. The pipeline is seeded (`random.seed(42)`), so re-running it reproduces these figures exactly — only the absolute dates slide forward. See [`project_metrics.md`](../project_metrics.md) for the tested reproducibility breakdown and the project's known limitations.

## 1. Initial exploration

The warehouse tracks 13 datasets (`data_inventory`) spanning 9 business domains, split across four criticality tiers: 4 **Regulatory Critical** (High regulatory_criticality — Customer Master, Transaction Ledger, Risk Exposure, General Ledger), 4 **Business Critical** (Medium), 3 **Operational Critical** (Low), and 2 **Reference Data** (Low). Against these 13 datasets, 30 controls (`control_rulebook`) run daily — but not every control applies to every dataset, so the real daily volume is **~45 control tests per day** (1,350 tests over the 30-day history, not a flat 13×30 = 390), averaging **~36 failures per day** at current pass rates.

That's the first uncomfortable number: a naive "run all checks, alert on every failure" approach would generate roughly 36 alerts a day across a team responsible for only 13 datasets. That's not a monitoring system, that's a pager that never stops.

## 2. First attempt: "just flag nulls and duplicates"

The obvious first instinct is a simple rule: scan for nulls, scan for duplicate keys, raise a ticket per failure. Three things break this quickly once you look at the actual data:

- **Alert fatigue.** At ~36 failed control tests/day, a flat alert-per-failure policy floods any team. There's no severity signal telling anyone which of those 36 failures is a Critical Control Failure on a regulatory dataset versus a Low-severity formatting drift on a reference table.
- **No trend visibility.** A single day's fail count says nothing about whether a dataset is degrading or recovering. Two datasets can have the same failure count today and completely different trajectories.
- **No accountability.** A raw alert has no owner, no SLA, no path to resolution — it's a fact, not a task. Of the 42 open exceptions in the current window, every single one needed a decision (who owns it, how fast does it need to close, is it actually getting worse) that a flat alert cannot supply on its own.

## 3. The pivot: governance, not detection

Detection (does a value fail a rule) is necessary but not sufficient. The real problem is **governance**: turning a flood of pass/fail signals into prioritized, owned, trending, auditable work. That reframing is what produced the 8-layer design (Detect → Assess → Prioritize → Remediate → Govern → Improve) instead of a bigger rules engine.

## 4. How each layer closes a specific gap

- **Detect** (`control_rulebook` + `control_test_results`) — the 30-control battery, unavoidable and necessary, but by itself it's the alert-fatigue problem above.
- **Assess** (`exceptions`) — collapses raw failures into three weighted exception types (Critical Control Failure, Repeated Control Failure, Regulatory Critical Issue). Currently 42 exceptions: 15 Critical Control Failures, 4 Regulatory Critical Issues, 15 High-severity Repeated Failures, 8 Medium — this is the first real compression of 36 raw daily failures into a short, prioritized list.
- **Prioritize** (`dq_watchlist`) — an early-warning layer that looks at *trend*, not just pass/fail: comparing early vs. recent 15-day windows across null rate, duplicate rate, and control-failure rate. Right now 8 of 13 datasets sit at "Watchlist" status and 5 at "Monitor" — meaning 13 of 13 datasets have some level of active visibility, none are silently degrading unnoticed.
- **Remediate** (`remediation_tickets`) — turns exceptions into owned tickets with severity-based SLAs (Critical=1 day, High=3, Medium=7, Low=14). Of 42 tickets, 21 currently carry an SLA breach flag, and resolved tickets closed in a mean 1.5 days — evidence the SLA structure is workable, but also that under-resourced follow-through is the real risk, not detection.
- **Govern** (`domain_trust_scores`) — rolls everything into one number per domain and enterprise-wide (currently 90.6, "Trusted" enterprise-wide), computed from 7-day control effectiveness minus watchlist/monitor penalties — a single, defensible headline metric for a CDO conversation instead of a spreadsheet of 1,350 raw test rows.
- **Improve** (`governance_maturity`) — a maturity score (currently 83.0, "Managed") intended to track the health of the *governance process itself* (SLA compliance, control coverage, automation), not just the data. Worth flagging honestly: two of its four inputs (`control_coverage`, `audit_completeness`) are currently fixed/degenerate values rather than measured signals — see [`INTERVIEW_PREP_OBJECTIONS.md`](../INTERVIEW_PREP_OBJECTIONS.md) Q13/Q15 for how that's addressed.

## 5. Validation: did the early-warning layer actually add value?

The clearest test: do datasets that end up with an open exception already have visibility, or are exceptions coming from datasets nobody was watching? Joining `exceptions` to `dq_watchlist`: **all 42 open exceptions (100%) belong to datasets already flagged Watchlist (19 exceptions, 8 datasets) or Monitor (23 exceptions, 5 datasets) — zero exceptions came from a dataset carrying a "Clear" status.** That's the honest validation claim: the early-warning layer is not catching things after the fact from blind spots — every dataset currently generating exceptions was already under active monitoring before those exceptions were logged. (What this data does *not* let us claim is a specific "5–10 days early" lead time, since `dq_watchlist` stores a point-in-time snapshot rather than dated daily history — see the Objection Prep doc for how that limitation is framed.)

## 6. Beyond the Obvious: What the Framework Revealed

The findings above validate the framework's design. Digging further — stress-testing the framework against its own output rather than just reading off its results — surfaced something more interesting: two opposite ways the framework's own scoring can mislead.

### The headline: 100% failure, still "Trusted"

Risk Exposure (DS-003) failed **every one of its 150 control tests over the full 30-day window** — a 100% fail rate, verified directly against `control_test_results`. Its domain (Risk) is nonetheless scored **94.1/100, "Trusted."** The reason: every one of DS-003's 5 assigned controls averages 94.8–95.1% `control_effectiveness` — high in absolute terms, but just under each rule's 98–100% threshold, so every test technically fails. The Data Trust Score formula averages continuous effectiveness rather than counting binary pass/fail outcomes, and DS-003 sits at "Monitor" status (a 1-point penalty), not "Watchlist" (3 points), so a total failure barely moves the score. A dataset can fail literally everything it's tested on and still be labeled trustworthy.

**Proposed fix, demonstrated (not just described) in `notebooks/01_governance_analysis.ipynb` Section 6b:** a binary floor — any dataset with a 100% fail rate in its trailing 7-day window is capped at "At Risk" regardless of average effectiveness. Applying this rule to live data flips Risk's domain score from **94.1 "Trusted" → 0.0 "At Risk"** — an executed SQL result, not a hypothetical.

A second, related fix addresses a different gap the same investigation surfaced: 25 dataset-rule pairs failed **every single day** of the 30-day window, and 22 of those 25 have exactly **one** remediation ticket total (`sql/v2_rebuild/SQL_COMPARISON.md` Edge Case 4) — nothing currently distinguishes a control that failed once from one that has failed continuously for a month. **Proposed fix, also demonstrated in the notebook (Section 6c):** a failure-streak escalation — a control failing 7+ consecutive days auto-escalates its severity one tier. Neither fix is implemented in `src/pipeline.py` in this pass (that file is intentionally left untouched); both are scoped, demonstrated design changes.

### The opposite failure mode: DS-012's false positive

Where DS-003 shows the framework being too *lenient*, Branch Reference (DS-012) shows it being too *strict*. Its duplicate rate moved from 0.0108% to 0.14% — **0.129 percentage points, trivial in absolute terms, on a dataset refreshed monthly** — and the framework reported that as **"Duplicate rate increased 1192.9%"**, the single largest movement on the entire watchlist (`sql/v2_rebuild/SQL_COMPARISON.md` Edge Case 1). Both DS-003 and DS-012 are the same class of problem — a formula that behaves badly at its edges — just at opposite ends: one masks total failure, the other amplifies a non-event into the loudest alert in the system.

Two things I initially got wrong here and corrected on a later verification pass, both worth keeping visible because they're ordinary analytical traps rather than exotic ones. First, I originally reported this as **+1,300%**, having recomputed it from the stored `duplicate_rate_early` column — not realising that column is rounded to 2dp for display (0.01). The pipeline's own stored reason, computed from the unrounded value, says 1,192.9%; my recomputation from the rounded intermediate was off by ~107 percentage points and silently contradicted the project's own [`COMPREHENSIVE_PROJECT_REPORT.md`](COMPREHENSIVE_PROJECT_REPORT.md), which had the right figure all along. Second, I attributed the behaviour to the `max(early, 0.01)` floor in the trend formula — but the true baseline (0.01083) sits *above* that floor, so it never fired. Checking all three signals across all 13 datasets, the floor doesn't engage anywhere in this data. The real cause is percentage-change-against-a-near-zero-baseline in general; the floor is a latent version of the same hazard, not the active one here.

### Which formula is "right"? Neither, cleanly

Recomputing every domain's trust score under a simple `(pass_count/total_tests)×100` compliance rate instead of the current weighted formula (`notebooks/01_governance_analysis.ipynb` Section 6d) produces a striking divergence: Risk (94.1 "Trusted") and Product (96.9 "Trusted") both show **0.0%** simple compliance; Customer (93.8 "Trusted") shows 13.6%. Other domains hold up better under both views (Finance: 98.9 vs. 90.6%). Neither formula is objectively correct — the current weighted formula tolerates near-miss thresholds and factors in trend data the simple version discards entirely (which is exactly what let DS-003 score "Trusted"), while the simple version is more alarming but throws away the early-warning signal that makes the Watchlist layer valuable in the first place. This is a real design tradeoff, not a bug to fix by picking a side. A fully redesigned governance maturity score — the framework's other composite metric — would draw on established models like **DAMA DMBOK** or the EDM Council's **DCAM**, rather than the current formula's two hardcoded constants (`control_coverage`, `audit_completeness`), which cannot move regardless of actual operational change.

### Root cause: why do Consistency controls fail so much more than other categories?

Verified live: Consistency controls fail 88.2% of tests, well above Accuracy (83.3%), Completeness (81.7%), and Timeliness (66.4%). By fail *rate* (not raw count), the worst offenders are `CONS-001` (Customer-Account Referential Integrity), `CONS-002` (Transaction-Account Consistency), and `CONS-007` (Customer Status Consistency) — all at 100% — with `CONS-004` (previously the most-cited "worst control" by raw fail count) actually only at 93.3% by rate; it just applies to more datasets/tests. Checking the actual rule descriptions in `src/pipeline.py`: `CONS-001`, `CONS-002`, and `CONS-007` are genuinely **cross-dataset referential checks** (an account must reference a valid customer, a transaction must reference a valid account, a status must agree across datasets) — structurally more fragile than single-table checks, since either side of the relationship can break the check independently. `CONS-005` (Risk Tier Consistency, also 100%) is the exception to this pattern — its description ("risk tier classification must be consistent with risk score bands") is a **within-dataset** field-to-field check on Risk Exposure alone, not a cross-table issue. So the honest root-cause claim is narrower than "all Consistency controls fail because they're cross-dataset": 3 of the 4 worst-failing rules are cross-dataset fragility, consistent with `docs/COMPREHENSIVE_PROJECT_REPORT.md` Finding 7's existing argument; `CONS-005` is a same-dataset threshold/calibration issue and shouldn't be lumped in with the other three.

### Why do High-severity tickets breach SLA more than Critical?

Verified live: High tickets (3-day SLA) breach at 73.3% (11/15); Critical (1-day SLA) breach at 52.6% (10/19) — counterintuitive, since High has the looser deadline. The resolution-time data explains why it isn't about speed: tickets that *do* get resolved average exactly 1.0 day regardless of severity. What differs is whether they get worked at all — only 13.3% of High tickets have ever reached Resolved, vs. 31.6% of Critical. The real story: teams triage Critical first, and High tickets sit unworked independent of their nominally more forgiving SLA window.

### Testing the architecture itself: is every layer pulling its weight?

The notebook's Section 7 counterfactual asks what would be lost if the Watchlist (Layer 5) didn't exist: DS-003's 100%-fail problem would still be visible directly in `control_test_results` (Layer 3 alone catches it), but DS-012's false-positive trend signal is a `dq_watchlist`-only concept — `control_test_results` has no null-rate or duplicate-rate columns at all, so that entire signal, and the "too strict" failure mode, would disappear without Layer 5. That distinguishes what Watchlist uniquely contributes from what's redundant with Detect.

Extending the same scrutiny to the other layers, one edge case per layer:
- **Layer 1 (Data Inventory):** a dataset not registered here is invisible to every downstream layer — governance coverage can silently drop if a new data source appears in production and nobody adds it.
- **Layer 2 (Control Rulebook):** the 30 rules were not checked for redundancy against each other — it's plausible some controls test overlapping conditions (e.g. a completeness check and a referential-integrity check on the same field pair), which would inflate the apparent control count without adding independent coverage.
- **Layer 3 (Control Testing):** the current design has no explicit handling for a control test itself failing to run (a timeout, a schema change) — the synthetic generator never produces this case, so the real-world failure mode is untested here.
- **Layer 4 (Exception Detection):** "Repeated Control Failure" triggers on ≥3-of-5 *recent* tests failing, which is why the 25 dataset-rule pairs failing all 30 days (above) don't get treated any differently from a rule that failed 3 of its last 5 tests — the promotion logic doesn't account for failure *duration*, only recent frequency.
- **Layer 6 (Remediation Workflow):** there is no reopen logic today — a ticket closed while its underlying control still fails just accumulates a new ticket next time, rather than escalating; this is the same gap behind the failure-streak fix proposed above.
- **Layer 7 (Governance Reporting):** enterprise-level aggregation can mask domain-level problems — DS-003's 100%-fail/94.1-Trusted contradiction is exactly this pattern one level down (domain masking dataset), and the same smoothing effect could in principle hide a domain-level problem behind a healthy enterprise number.
- **Layer 8 (Monthly Governance Review):** per `docs/COMPREHENSIVE_PROJECT_REPORT.md`'s own description, Layer 8's outputs (the static report + dashboard Page 5) are computed from the same tables Layer 7 already aggregates (`domain_trust_scores`, `governance_maturity`) — it adds board-narrative translation, not new analytical content, and would be the first layer to cut under resource constraints. A monthly cadence also means a problem discovered on day 2 of the cycle isn't formally reported for up to 30 days — the live dashboard (Layer 7) is what actually closes that gap day-to-day.

### One more design tradeoff worth naming: the trust score's built-in lag

The Data Trust Score averages `control_effectiveness` over a trailing 7-day window. That smooths daily noise, but it also means a brand-new failure (a source-system migration, a broken ETL job) takes up to 7 days to meaningfully move the score — the same averaging behavior that let DS-003's near-threshold effectiveness mask a 100% fail rate also means the score is structurally slow to react to sudden new problems. A shorter window would be more responsive at the cost of more day-to-day score volatility; the current 7-day choice is a tradeoff, not an oversight.
